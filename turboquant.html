The Maths Behind TurboQuant

Classical results from probability, information theory, and geometry that explain why TurboQuant can compress KV-cache vectors aggressively while preserving the signal attention needs. Each section starts with intuition, then states the mathematics more carefully.

30-second view

Rotate the KV vector, quantise the rotated coordinates efficiently, then keep a compact sign sketch of the residual so query-time inner products can be corrected.

What is exact?

The arc-cosine identity, the Gaussian rate-distortion function, and the exact coordinate law on the sphere are exact. Several other statements are asymptotic or benchmark approximations.

What is the point?

This is not “no approximation.” It is approximation in the right coordinates, with concentration and variance control that make the compression useful inside attention.

TurboQuant in five steps
  1. Generate shared random operators \(R\) and \(J\) from a seed.
  2. Rotate the KV vector: \(y = Rx\).
  3. Quantise \(y\) coordinatewise with a fixed scalar grid to obtain Stage 1.
  4. Form the residual \(r = x - R^\top \tilde y\), then store \(\mathrm{sign}(Jr)\) as Stage 2.
  5. At query time, combine the Stage 1 reconstruction with a calibrated estimator from \(\mathrm{sign}(Jr)\) and \(Jq\) to approximate \(\langle x,q\rangle\).

01

Concentration of Measure

Intuition Imagine throwing a dart at a beach ball. In 2D, the dart could land anywhere. In 3D, slightly more surface area is near the equator. In very high dimension, most of the sphere lies close to an equatorial band relative to any fixed direction, and deviations away from that band become exponentially unlikely. High dimensionality becomes an asset: random projections behave more predictably as the dimension grows.

This is the foundational geometric fact. If \(u\) is uniform on the sphere \(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\) and \(x\) is fixed, then the scalar projection \(\langle u, x \rangle\) has a sharply concentrated distribution. For large \(d\), it is well approximated by a Gaussian with variance \(\|x\|^2/d\):

Concentration on the sphere · asymptotic Gaussian + exact tail bound \(\langle u, x \rangle \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\,\frac{\|x\|^2}{d}\right)\)

Standard spherical concentration gives a subgaussian tail of the form \(\;\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\langle u,x\rangle\right| > \varepsilon\right) \leq 2\exp\!\left(-c\,\frac{d\,\varepsilon^2}{\|x\|^2}\right)\) for an absolute constant \(c>0\).

The tail probability decays exponentially in \(d\). A deviation that is a 10% mistake in 10D is essentially impossible in 1000D — the probability falls by a factor of \(e^{99}\). This is not intuitive from everyday experience; it is purely a high-dimensional phenomenon.

V1 How fast projections concentrate as dimension grows

Distribution of \(\langle u, x\rangle\) for random unit \(u\). Orange dashed lines: theoretical \(\pm\sigma = \pm\|x\|/\sqrt{d}\). Drag d up — watch the distribution collapse inward. The concentration is already visible at d=64; by d=256 the spread is tiny.

Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings Concentration suggests that most rotated coordinates \((Rx)_i\) fall in a predictable \(O(\|x\|/\sqrt d)\) range. That makes a shared scalar grid plausible and reduces the need for per-coordinate metadata, though practical implementations still depend on how norms are handled and what clipping level is chosen.
02

CLT & Beta Distribution of Rotated Coordinates

Intuition A key vector in an LLM might have most of its energy concentrated in just a few dimensions — like a microphone that mostly picks up bass and ignores treble. After a random rotation, that same information is spread much more evenly across coordinates. The coordinates are exchangeable, each has the same marginal law, and in high dimension they behave close to weakly dependent Gaussian samples. That is the regime where a single scalar quantiser becomes attractive.

Each coordinate of \(Rx\) is a linear combination of all \(d\) original coordinates. By the Central Limit Theorem, sums of many weakly-correlated terms converge to Gaussian. More precisely, the normalised squared coordinates follow a Beta distribution that concentrates around equal energy per dimension:

Distribution of rotated coordinates · exact marginal for squares, asymptotic Gaussian for coordinates \((Rx)_i \;\overset{d}{\approx}\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\frac{\|x\|^2}{d}\right) \quad \text{for large } d\)

Exact: \(\quad\frac{(Rx)_i^2}{\|x\|^2} \;\sim\; \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{d-1}{2}\right)\)\n
Equivalent rescaling: \(\;\frac{d\,(Rx)_i^2}{\|x\|^2} \sim d\cdot \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\,\tfrac{d-1}{2}\right)\)

and coordinates \((Rx)_i\) are approximately independent.

Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings Near-i.i.d. Gaussian coordinates → the same Lloyd-Max quantiser grid is near-optimal for every dimension independently. No per-block scale factors, no codebook to store. At \(d=4096\) (a typical head dimension), the approximation is extremely tight.
03

Rate-Distortion Theory

Intuition There is a hard speed limit on compression — like the speed of light for information. Shannon proved that for a Gaussian signal, the minimum possible error at \(R\) bits per sample is exactly \(\sigma^2 \cdot 2^{-2R}\) — no scheme, no matter how clever, can do better. A simple scalar quantiser applied to a Gaussian is near-optimal in the high-rate regime; the classical \(\pi e/6\) factor corresponds to about 1.53 dB excess distortion in that asymptotic setting. The rotation step is meant to make coordinate marginals substantially more Gaussian-like so this scalar quantisation picture becomes a better approximation. Without rotation, the same quantiser can be materially worse.

Shannon's rate-distortion theorem gives the fundamental compression limit. For a Gaussian source with variance \(\sigma^2\), the minimum MSE achievable at \(R\) bits per sample is:

Shannon rate-distortion bound (Gaussian source) · exact source bound \(D^*(R) = \sigma^2 \cdot 2^{-2R}\)

A scalar (per-sample) Lloyd-Max quantiser on a Gaussian source achieves: \(\;D_{\text{scalar}} \leq \dfrac{\pi e}{6} \cdot D^*(R) \approx D^*(R) + 1.53\;\text{dB}\)

The \(\pi e/6\) gap is the classical high-rate scalar-quantisation constant for Gaussian-like sources; it should be read as an asymptotic benchmark rather than a finite-rate theorem for every setting.

For a non-Gaussian source (spiky activation vectors), scalar quantisation can be far worse. The role of rotation is to move the coordinates toward the Gaussian regime where this constant-gap guarantee becomes a better approximation. That is a strong signal-level argument, though not by itself an end-to-end model-quality guarantee.

Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings At 3.5 bits/dim, the scalar quantiser can operate near the Gaussian signal-level limit. That suggests little compression inefficiency remains at the coordinate level, even though downstream model accuracy still depends on how these errors propagate through attention.

Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma

Intuition You have a million points in a million-dimensional space. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma says they can be mapped into a much lower-dimensional space while approximately preserving all pairwise distances, with high probability over a random projection. The key message is that the target dimension depends logarithmically on the number of points and does not scale directly with the ambient dimension; the exact dimension still depends strongly on the desired distortion and constants in the bound.
Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma · high-probability embedding guarantee For any \(n\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(\varepsilon \in (0,1)\), there exists a linear map \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k = O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}\right)\) such that for all pairs \(i,j\): \[(1-\varepsilon)\|x_i - x_j\|^2 \;\leq\; \|f(x_i)-f(x_j)\|^2 \;\leq\; (1+\varepsilon)\|x_i-x_j\|^2\] And a random Gaussian matrix \(J\) with entries \(\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/k)\) is such a map with high probability. Note: \(d\) does not appear in the bound on \(k\).

One clean proof route combines three ingredients: norm concentration for one vector, a reduction from pairwise distances to norm preservation, and a union bound over all pairs. The demos below emphasise those ingredients, while Point 1 is best viewed as geometric intuition rather than a complete proof on its own.

Point 1 — High dimensions are mostly "equator" → why a random projection works at all

Pick two random unit vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Their dot product is \(\langle u, v \rangle = \cos\theta\). In 2D, \(\theta\) is uniformly random — you'd expect angles ranging from 0° to 180°. In high dimensions, almost all random unit vectors are nearly orthogonal to each other: \(\theta \approx 90°\). The surface area of the sphere concentrates near the equatorial hyperplane relative to any fixed direction.

This near-orthogonality is why you can draw Gaussian matrix entries without careful design — random directions don't "pile up" on each other, so a random projection sees the data evenly from many independent directions.

→ Answers: why does a random projection work at all?
JL-A Angle between random unit vectors — converges to 90° in high dimensions

Histogram of angles (in degrees) between random pairs of unit vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). Drag d up — the distribution collapses tightly onto 90°. In 2D it's uniform; by d=100 nearly every pair is almost orthogonal.

Point 2 — Projection length concentrates → the (1±ε) accuracy guarantee

Project a vector onto \(k\) independent random Gaussian directions and compute the average squared projection. This is like asking \(k\) independent surveyors to estimate the same length — the average concentrates around the truth as \(k\) grows. Formally, \(\|Jx\|^2\) is a scaled chi-squared random variable with \(k\) degrees of freedom, and its relative deviation falls within \(\pm\varepsilon\) of \(\|x\|^2\) with probability at least:

\[\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\|Jx\|^2 - \|x\|^2\right| > \varepsilon\|x\|^2\right) \;\leq\; 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k\varepsilon^2}{4}\right)\]

This is the core inequality of the proof — the actual distance-preservation guarantee for a single pair.

→ Answers: how accurate is the projection for one pair?
JL-B Distribution of projected distance ratio ‖Jx−Jy‖²/‖x−y‖² for one pair

Each sample: one random projection matrix \(J\). X-axis: ratio of projected to true squared distance (should be 1.0 if perfect). Orange lines: \(1\pm\varepsilon\) bounds. Increase k — the distribution collapses around 1. The fraction outside the bounds matches the theoretical bound.

Point 3 — Union bound over n² pairs → the k = O(log n / ε²) dimension count

For \(n\) points there are \(\binom{n}{2} \approx n^2/2\) pairs. We need every pair to stay within \((1\pm\varepsilon)\). From Point 2, each pair fails with probability \(\leq 2e^{-k\varepsilon^2/4}\). By the union bound, the probability that any pair fails is at most:

\[\frac{n^2}{2} \cdot 2e^{-k\varepsilon^2/4} = n^2 \cdot e^{-k\varepsilon^2/4}\]

Set this \(\leq \delta\) (say \(\delta = 0.01\)) and solve for \(k\):

\[k \;\geq\; \frac{4}{\varepsilon^2}\ln\!\frac{n^2}{\delta} = \frac{8\log n}{\varepsilon^2} + \frac{4\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^2} = O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^2}\right)\]

The \(\log n\) comes from surviving \(n^2\) pairs. The \(1/\varepsilon^2\) comes from Point 2's concentration rate. The original dimension \(d\) never appears — it is absorbed by the normalisation of \(J\)'s entries.

For \(n=1,000,000\) and \(\varepsilon=0.1\), the simple union-bound estimate shown here lands in the tens of thousands of dimensions rather than a few hundred; sharper constants can improve this, but not to \(\sim 400\) under the displayed bound.

The three facts compose into the lemma

The lemma is exactly these three statements in sequence:

  • [Point 1] A random Gaussian matrix is a valid candidate — random directions are nearly orthogonal, so the map doesn't wildly distort the data.
  • [Point 2] For any single pair, the projection preserves their distance within \((1\pm\varepsilon)\) with probability \(\geq 1 - 2e^{-k\varepsilon^2/4}\).
  • [Point 3] Choosing \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\) makes the failure probability summed over all \(n^2\) pairs go below any fixed \(\delta\).
Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings JL explains why a shared random projection can preserve enough geometry for downstream estimation. It does not by itself prove that one sign bit per row is sufficient, and it should not be used as the main justification for the one-bit residual estimator. Its role here is motivational: random projections can preserve geometry surprisingly well, while the actual one-bit correction argument comes from the arc-cosine identity and the estimator analysis below.
05

Arc-Cosine Identity for Gaussian Projections

Intuition Flip a coin whose bias is determined by the angle between two vectors. The probability the coin comes up heads equals \(1 - \theta/\pi\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the vectors. That identity is exact for Gaussian hyperplanes. A single random bit therefore carries angular information, and many such bits can be aggregated into a calibrated similarity estimate.

This is the sharpest result in the pipeline: an exact identity, valid for any dimension \(d\) and any nonzero vectors \(r,q\). For a random Gaussian vector \(j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\):

Arc-cosine identity (exact, any d) \(\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathrm{sign}(j^\top r) = \mathrm{sign}(j^\top q)\right) = 1 - \frac{\theta(r,q)}{\pi}\)

where \(\theta(r,q) = \arccos\!\left(\frac{\langle r,q\rangle}{\|r\|\|q\|}\right)\). And the key mixed expectation:

\(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{sign}(j^\top r)\cdot(j^\top q)\right] = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\dfrac{\langle r,\,q\rangle}{\|r\|}\)

The second identity makes Stage 2 work. Store \(\mathrm{sign}(Jr)\) (one bit per row of \(J\)). At query time, compute \(J_i^\top q\) from the same shared seed, multiply, and average. The result is proportional to \(\langle r, q\rangle\), with a known calibration factor and finite-sample variance.

Proof sketch: Let \(a = j^\top r/\|r\|\) and write \(j^\top q = \rho\|q\| a + \epsilon\) where \(\rho=\cos\theta\) and \(\epsilon\perp a\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{sign}(a)\cdot j^\top q] = \rho\|q\|\mathbb{E}[|a|] = \rho\|q\|\sqrt{2/\pi}\).

Calibration note The mixed expectation is proportional to \(\langle r,q\rangle\), not equal to it directly. Multiplying the sample average by \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\|r\|\) removes the known factor. That distinction matters when calling the estimator “unbiased.”
V5 Arc-cosine identity — theoretical curve vs empirical sign-agreement rate

Green curve: exact formula \(p(\theta)=1-\theta/\pi\). Blue dots: empirical sign-agreement frequency at 20 equally-spaced angles using random Gaussian projections. The agreement is exact in expectation — more samples = dots collapse onto the curve.

What this buys you in Stage 2 After rescaling by the known factor \(\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\|r\|\), this gives an unbiased estimator of \(\langle r, q\rangle\). In practice, Stage 2 is best understood as a calibrated correction term: the rescaled estimator has the correct mean, and its accuracy improves with the number of projection rows.
06

Law of Large Numbers — Variance Decay

Intuition You are trying to estimate a building's height. One estimate from a passerby: noisy. Ten estimates averaged: better. A hundred: accurate. Each projection row in Stage 2 is one noisy but calibrated measurement of the residual inner product. Average enough of them and the estimator concentrates around its expectation. The law of large numbers matters twice here: high dimension stabilises rotated coordinates, and larger sketch size stabilises the residual correction.
QJL estimator — exact mean, variance decays as 1/m \(\hat{\ell}_m = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \mathrm{sign}(j_i^\top r)\cdot(j_i^\top q)\)

\(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\ell}_m] = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\dfrac{\langle r,q\rangle}{\|r\|} \qquad\text{(unbiased up to known constant)}\)

\(\mathrm{Var}[\hat{\ell}_m] = \dfrac{C}{m} \qquad\text{(shrinks as }1/m\text{, by LLN)}\)
V6 QJL estimator variance decays as 1/m (log-log scale)

Blue: empirical variance of \(\hat\ell_m\) vs number of sign bits \(m\). Orange: \(1/m\) reference line. Log-log scale so slope = −1 if variance ∝ 1/m. The slope matches theory. This tells you exactly how many sign bits you need for a given accuracy target.

Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings TurboQuant can devote a small auxiliary sketch to the residual because Stage 1 has already removed most of the signal energy. The required number of sign rows is therefore a variance-budget question: more rows reduce noise like \(1/m\), but the right operating point is ultimately empirical.
07

Data-Obliviousness & Shared Randomness

Intuition Two people can agree in advance on a shared recipe — say, a random seed — and later use it independently without further communication. TurboQuant uses this idea: \(R\) and \(J\) are regenerated from shared randomness rather than stored per vector. That does not make them literally free in every systems sense, but it does mean their description cost can be amortised across many vectors instead of paid repeatedly.

Because \(R\) and \(J\) are drawn independently of any single input \(x\), the same transforms can be reused across vectors. The sign sketch has a fixed alphabet \(\{-1,+1\}^m\), while the quantised rotated coordinates can often be described with shared grid parameters plus a small amount of norm information.

What must be stored per KV vector \(\underbrace{\mathrm{quant}(Rx)}_{b\;\text{bits/dim}} \;\|\; \underbrace{\mathrm{sign}(Jr)}_{1\;\text{bit/dim}} \;\|\; \underbrace{\|x\|}_{\text{1 scalar}}\)

\(R,\, J\): stored once as a random seed — \(O(1)\) bytes total regardless of model size or context length.

Connection to TurboQuant & attention savings The systems appeal is that shared randomness reduces per-vector metadata. At aggressive bitrates, saving even a fraction of a bit per dimension matters. The exact memory win versus alternative schemes depends on implementation details, but the direction of the advantage is clear: fewer per-vector side parameters means a larger share of memory goes to signal itself.

References & provenance

These are the classical results the explainer leans on. Listing them explicitly makes it easier to distinguish established theorems from TurboQuant-specific interpretation.

  • Spherical concentration / Lévy-type phenomena. Standard high-dimensional probability results for random points on the sphere and Lipschitz observables.
  • Coordinate laws on the sphere. For \(u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb S^{d-1})\), the squared coordinate \(u_i^2\) has a Beta distribution; Gaussian approximations emerge as \(d\to\infty\).
  • Shannon rate-distortion theory. For a Gaussian source, \(D^*(R)=\sigma^2 2^{-2R}\) is exact.
  • High-rate scalar quantisation. The \(\pi e/6\) constant is the classical asymptotic gap between optimal scalar quantisation and the Gaussian rate-distortion limit.
  • Johnson–Lindenstrauss embeddings. Random projections preserve pairwise geometry with target dimension \(O(\log n/\varepsilon^2)\).
  • Random hyperplane / arc-cosine identity. Gaussian sign projections recover angular similarity exactly in expectation.
  • Monte Carlo variance scaling. Averaging independent rows drives estimator variance down like \(1/m\).
